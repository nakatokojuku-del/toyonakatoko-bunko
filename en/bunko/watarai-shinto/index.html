What is Watarai Shinto?— the foundation of learning at Nakatoko-juku

In the bustle of daily life, there are moments when you seem about to lose sight of “your own centre”. At such times, a single path handed down for more than a thousand years can offer a quiet clue. The age of the gods is not long ago; it is here, in the very midst of this day. That is what Watarai Shinto (度會神道) tells us. It is the Shinto handed down at the Outer Shrine (Gekū) of Ise Jingū, and is also called Ise Shinto.

What does it teach?

Watarai Shinto places at the centre of the world the primordial kami who dwell at the beginning of heaven and earth — Ame-no-Minakanushi-no-kami (天御中主神) and Kuni-no-Tokotachi-no-mikoto (国常立尊). And it teaches that “the way of the kami (kamunagara no michi) is the way of Japan itself”.

The passage that best conveys its heart is this:

神代その儘今にありて、天の下平らけく泰らけくならざると云ふ事なし。The age of the gods remains, just as it is, here and now; there is nothing under heaven that is not made peaceful and serene.— Nakatomi-no-harae Mizuho-shō (中臣祓瑞穂鈔)

The age of the gods is not an old tale. It is here, just as it is, in the very midst of this day — this is the gaze of Watarai Shinto, and the single point on which Nakatoko-juku stands.

The foundational texts — the Five Books of Shinto

Watarai Shinto takes as its foundation the texts known as the Shintō Gobusho (神道五部書, “the Five Books of Shinto”). One of them is the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki (倭姫命世記), which tells of the origins of Ise Jingū. Although their colophons claim great antiquity, research suggests that they were in fact compiled in the Kamakura period by Watarai Yukitada (度會行忠), a priest of the Outer Shrine, and others. Later, in the period of the Northern and Southern Courts, Watarai Ieyuki (度會家行, 1256–1351) drew on them to bring Ise Shinto (Watarai Shinto) together into a single system.

The restorer — Deguchi Nobuyoshi

In the Edo period, the man who revived this way as a field of learning was Deguchi Nobuyoshi (出口延佳, also known as Watarai Nobuyoshi; born in 1615), a gon-negi (assistant priest) of the Outer Shrine. Nobuyoshi taught that “Shinto is the way of Japan”, and opened the Toyomiyazaki Bunko (豐宮崎文庫) as a place where anyone could study. The ideals of Nakatoko-juku and the Toyonakatoko Bunko carry on Nobuyoshi’s aspiration.

How we learn at Nakatoko-juku

At Nakatoko-juku, we let Watarai Shinto settle into the hara (肚) by “reading, copying and listening”: sodoku (reading the classics aloud), copying by hand, and lectures — learning that touches the age of the gods not only through the understanding of the head, but with the whole body.

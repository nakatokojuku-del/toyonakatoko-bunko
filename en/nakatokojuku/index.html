Nakatoko-juku — remembering the eternal centre of the Japanese

Nakatoko-juku (中常塾)　Founder: Shigeki Matsuura　Opened on 25 May 2026

The age of the gods is not a distant past. It is here, in the very midst of your day today. Nakatoko-juku is a place to touch that age once more through the study of Watarai Shinto (度會神道, the Shinto tradition of the Outer Shrine of Ise) and the practice of wagi® (和儀) — a place to rediscover the hara of the Japanese.
Okazaki lies to the ushitora, the north-east, of the Grand Shrine of Ise. The founder was taught that the story of one family, which set out from Izumo, came to rest here at the end of a long road. As one who carries that story, he continues, in daily practice, the way of the kami that has come down from before the time of Emperor Jinmu. It is also a place where we savour that story together, and go on writing what comes next.

造

Five kinds of making — circles that widen from one person’s hara

What will you find here? In a word: five kinds of “making” (tsukuri) that begin in your own hara. From the inside outwards, from one person’s body to the nation, they are joined like concentric circles on a single piece of ground.

Making the person
人つくり
Through sodoku (reading the classics aloud) and practice, your own hara, body and voice come into order. Everything begins here.
Making a life
人生つくり
The study of Watarai Shinto opens your eyes to read your own path anew, in the light of the myths.
Making a village
邑つくり
A gathering of kindred spirits modelled on the old Ise-kō (伊勢講, confraternities for pilgrimage to Ise), the Nakatoko-kō (中常講), is beginning to grow. The morning letter, the Nakatoko Dayori, is its doorway.
Making a town
街つくり
Ties with the Noh theatre here in Okazaki and with the festivals of this land spread out beyond the learning.
Making the nation
國造り
A path opens that leads to offerings to the Grand Shrine and to service at its festivals. Making the nation is not a distant ideal; it lies on the line that runs on from one person’s making of the hara.

That said, this is not a place for knitted brows. Taking the myths as our model, we make one story together — savouring words in the mouth, savouring them in the body, savouring them over sacred sake and at the feast. That accumulated savouring becomes, just as it is, the five kinds of making. The one who writes the next scene of this story may be you, reading this now.

名

The origin of the name “Nakatoko”

Our name comes from the writings of Deguchi Nobuyoshi (出口延佳, 1615–1690), a priest of the Outer Shrine of Ise. In his lectures to his disciples on the Age of the Gods chapters of the Nihon Shoki, Nobuyoshi said:

「中常ノ道ヲ神道トセルノ深意」 “The deep meaning of taking the way of nakatoko as Shinto.”
— Deguchi Nobuyoshi (Watarai Nobuyoshi), Jindai Kōjutsu-shō (神代講述鈔), as recorded by Yamamoto Hirotari

Nakatoko (中常) stands on the practical ground that the present is itself eternity. This single point that is now is, just as it is, eternity — not going back to the past, nor waiting for some day to come; the very place where you stand now becomes your footing.

The two characters come from Ame-no-Minakanushi-no-kami (天御中主神), who is one and the same as Kuni-no-Tokotachi-no-mikoto (國常立尊) under another name. Like the Pole Star (hokushin) at the centre of the heavens: the centre, eternal, the unmoving axis. We took as our name the words of Nobuyoshi, who called that way “Shinto”. ▶ More: the name “Nakatoko” (Japanese)

The manuscript of the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki in our library names the Shin-no-mihashira (心御柱, the “heart pillar” at the centre of the shrine) as well: in a note to the account of the founding of the Inner Shrine (leaf 21), the great pillar is called “also Ame-no-mihashira, also Shin-no-mihashira”. The pillar of the central axis of heaven — the pillar of nakatoko: the ground of our name survives in the text itself.

源

The founder’s roots and Izawa-no-miya

The founder, Matsuura, was taught that his father’s line goes back in a single lineage to the Tomi clan (登美氏) of the Izumo people.

Source The Tomi clan of the Izumo people
Of the same clan as Tomi-no-Nagasunehiko, chief of the earthly kami (kunitsukami), who was defeated by Emperor Jinmu.
Izawatomi-no-kami (伊佐波登美神), who built Izawa-no-miya, also belongs to this line.
The Abe clan of Ōshū A clan carrying the blood of the Tomi, who worshipped the Pole Star and were called the kings of the north-east.
In the Former Nine Years’ War (1051–62), Abe no Munetō escaped to Matsuura in Kyushu.
The present surname, Matsuura In Matsuura, the family joined the local Matsuura clan. This is the founder’s present surname.
Leaf twenty-five of the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki manuscript held by our library states plainly that, at the command of Yamatohime-no-mikoto, Izawatomi-no-kami built Izawa-no-miya. The ancestral kami of the Tomi — the source of the founder’s father’s line — appears by name in this text. A book in which one can point and say “here is my ancestor’s name” is now in our hands.
Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki manuscript, leaf 25 — the name “(Iza)watomi-no-kami” can be seen at the beginning
A map of the journey — from Izumo to Okazaki

Every Japanese person carries in their blood a story that has continued since the age of the gods. Place the stories you heard from your grandparents and parents on a single map, and an unexpected road appears. What I show here is the story I was taught, transferred just as it is onto a map of Japan. Setting out from Izumo and defeated in Yamato, it passes through Tsugaru, Koromogawa, Kyoto, Ōshima in Tsukushi, Matsuura, Minami village in Hiroshima and Shimonoseki, returns to Taisha Kizuki in Izumo, where my mother was born, and now reaches Okazaki.

Gold double circle (1 and 10): Taisha Kizuki, Izumo — the place of departure, and the place where I was born (map labels in Japanese)

As you can see, this is not a lineage of victors. It is the road of people who lost the land they lived on with every defeat, and yet never stopped walking. The plum-blossom poem of Abe no Munetō handed down in the Taiheiki; Akita Sanesue, who spent nearly thirty years at Asama in Ise compiling the family tree anew — the whole of the road is gathered in a reading in the Toyonakatoko Bunko. Of course, the events of distant ages are stories I was taught, and not everything can be proven. A guide to drawing your own family’s map is included there as well.

Offerings — kakechikara and sacred sake

Leaf twenty-five of the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki records the origins of kakechikara (懸税, ears of new rice hung as an offering) and of sacred sake (御神酒, omiki). Nakatoko-juku’s two offering projects find their textual ground in this one passage.

The ears of rice held in the beak of a manazuru crane are hung in offering before the Great Kami — the origin of kakechikara
From those ears, Otohime, daughter of Ōhatanushi, is bidden to brew clear sake, and the sacred meal is offered — the origin of sacred sake

学

The study of Watarai Shinto, and wagi

Wagi practice on the Noh stage of the Okazaki Castle Ninomaru

At the heart of Nakatoko-juku lies the study of Watarai Shinto, the stream of Shinto systematised by the Watarai family of the Outer Shrine of Ise. The founder, Matsuura, encountered Watarai Shinto in 2008 and has pursued it ever since.

The manuscript of the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki held by the Toyonakatoko Bunko is a copy of this foundational classic of Watarai Shinto collated by Deguchi Nobuyoshi himself. That is why we chose it as the first volume of our library.

Wagi (和儀) was developed by the kyōgen actor Sensaburō Shigeyama, born and raised in a family of kyōgen actors going back generations. It was created so that, through its practice, Japanese people today can recover the bodily techniques of the original Japanese way of standing, sitting and moving, as handed down in Noh and Kyōgen.

Believing that “the secret to mastering the way of nakatoko lies in Noh and Kyōgen”, Matsuura knocked at the door of Noh more than ten years ago. At that time he was unexpectedly given the chance to offer dance and chant on the Noh stage of the Grand Shrine. He went on exploring on his own; ten years later, Sensaburō-sensei developed wagi. Two years after that, Matsuura encountered wagi, travelled to Kyoto to learn from Sensaburō-sensei, and in September 2025 was granted the qualification of shihan (master instructor). At Nakatoko-juku, wagi is the core programme for learning the way of nakatoko.

庫

Nakatoko-juku and the Toyonakatoko Bunko

The Toyonakatoko Bunko (豐中常文庫) is a library that Nakatoko-juku opened to copy the Watarai Shinto texts in its keeping into digital form and to hand them widely to readers today. Its name carries on the spirit of the Toyomiyazaki Bunko (豐宮崎文庫), which Deguchi Nobuyoshi founded at the foot of the Outer Shrine.

The founder’s offering projects, his sacred sake and the roots of his family — the origins of every one of them are recorded in a single passage (leaf twenty-five) of the first volume, the Yamatohime-no-mikoto Seiki. The library is the place where we open to the world the physical ground of Nakatoko-juku’s story.
